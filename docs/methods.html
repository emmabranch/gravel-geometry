Data and methods

Where the geometry comes from, how it was checked, what this site calculates, and the assumptions behind it.

Where the numbers come from

Every number was typed in by hand from the manufacturer’s own published geometry chart, read in a web browser on 21 November 2026. Nothing was scraped, and nothing comes from third-party geometry databases. The text of each chart, as read, is kept in the repository (data/chart-captures/), so any number can be traced back.

  • 24 bikes from 19 brands, chosen to cover current race, all-round, and adventure gravel bikes, including Canadian brands (Norco and Devinci).
  • Every published size. Where a bike has adjustable geometry (Giant and Liv flip chips, the Cervélo Trail Mixer fork insert), each setting has its own rows.
  • Units: charts in centimetres (Trek and Cannondale) were converted to millimetres. Cannondale publishes its chart as an image, which was read by eye.
  • Left out: the Rocky Mountain Solo (not listed on the Canadian site when checked), Kona, Ridley, and Niner (gravel pages would not load), and the Surly Grappler (closer to a mountain bike).

Manufacturers change their charts, sometimes without notice. Check the source before buying anything.

raw = FileAttachment("data/gravel_geometry.csv").csv({typed: true})
sources = Array.from(
  new Map(raw.map(d => [d.bike_id, d])).values(),

    Accessed: d.accessed,
    url: d.source_url,

Categories

Race, all-round, and adventure are this site’s labels, not the manufacturers’. They follow how each manufacturer describes the bike’s intended use. They are a rough grouping for filtering, and several bikes sit near the edges: the Norco Search, for example, is labelled adventure but is long and low, with a slack head angle.

How the data were checked

Geometry charts contain redundant numbers: several measurements can be recalculated from the others. Where a recalculated value disagreed with the published one, the typed number was re-read against the chart. The checks run in R/check_geometry.R.

Check How Result
Top tube effective top tube = reach + stack ÷ tan(seat angle) Within 12 mm for every size except three OPEN WI.DE. sizes, whose “horizontal” top tube is measured differently from its own reach and stack
Wheelbase rebuilt from stack, reach, head angle, fork offset, BB drop, and chainstay Within 10 mm for every size with a published offset
Front centre published front centre plus chainstay against wheelbase Within 6 mm except the Santa Cruz Stigmata (about 7 mm short), which probably measures front centre differently
Trail recalculated against published trail, 72 sizes from 11 bikes Mean difference −0.9 mm (SD 2.5). Orbea runs about 5 mm higher because its chart assumes a smaller tyre
Size order stack and reach should not shrink as sizes grow True for every bike

What this site calculates

All lengths are in millimetres, angles in degrees. The bottom bracket (BB) is the origin.

Wheel radius. A 700c rim has a 622 mm bead seat diameter. The tyre’s height is taken as equal to its width, so with the reference 45 mm tyre, R = 311 + 45 = 356 mm. The Salsa Cutthroat uses its stock 29 × 2.25” tyre (about 57 mm).

Trail. With head angle θ and fork offset f:

\[\text{trail} = \frac{R \cos\theta - f}{\sin\theta}\]

Mechanical trail is trail × sin θ: the same gap measured at right angles to the steering axis, which is the lever arm the rider actually feels. Wheel flop is trail × sin θ × cos θ: how far the front of the bike drops as the bars turn.

Front centre (horizontal, BB to front axle):

\[\text{front centre} = \text{reach} + \frac{\text{stack} - \text{BB drop}}{\tan\theta} + \frac{f}{\sin\theta}\]

Fork offset, where it isn’t published (Canyon, Devinci, and 3T). Rearranging the wheelbase equation gives the offset from the published wheelbase, chainstay, BB drop, stack, reach, and head angle. For the 118 sizes where offset is published, this lands within 2 mm for 115 of them (mean difference 0.0 mm, SD 0.6). These estimates are marked “est.” on the site. The Santa Cruz Stigmata’s offset comes from an FAQ answer (45 mm) that does not fit its chart, so the chart-based estimate (51 to 52 mm) is used instead.

Toe clearance is the straight-line distance from the BB to the front axle, minus the tyre radius, minus a 172.5 mm crank. A typical shoe reaches about 90 to 110 mm ahead of the pedal axle, so smaller figures suggest toe overlap in tight, slow turns.

Stack-to-reach ratio is stack divided by reach.

Drawings

Bikes are drawn from the same numbers, to the same scale, with the BB at the origin. Tubes are straight lines between published points, so curved tubes and bent forks are simplified. Where a chart leaves out head tube or seat tube length (only the Factor ELARA), the drawing uses the median relationship across the other bikes (head tube about stack minus 434 mm, seat tube about 0.86 × stack), and says so.

The rider model

The Rider page estimates where a rider’s joints sit on each bike. It is an estimate from average body proportions, not a bike fit.

  • Presets use measured standing height from Statistics Canada’s Canadian Health Measures Survey, cycle 2 (2009 to 2011): mean height of women aged 20 to 39 was 163.0 cm and 40 to 59 was 161.9 cm (preset 162 cm); men 177.7 cm and 174.7 cm (preset 176 cm). The shorter and taller presets are the 5th percentile for women aged 20 to 39 (153.3 cm) and the 95th percentile for men aged 20 to 39 (187.7 cm). This is the most recent survey cycle with published height tables by sex and age.
  • Body segments are fixed fractions of height from Drillis and Contini (1966), as tabulated by Winter: thigh 0.245, shin 0.246, ankle height 0.039, upper arm 0.186, forearm 0.146, hand 0.108, shoulder height 0.818, and hip (greater trochanter) height 0.530.
  • Inseam, when not entered, is 0.475 × height, from measured crotch height and stature in NIOSH’s law enforcement anthropometry survey (men 841.0 / 1776.1 mm; women 793.9 / 1651.1 mm).
  • Saddle height is 0.883 × inseam from the bottom bracket to the top of the saddle (the LeMond method), along the seat tube’s angle. The method does not account for crank length.
  • Hip joint sits 0.033 × height above the top of the saddle. This value puts the knee at about 30° at the LeMond height for an average rider, the middle of the usual 25 to 35° target, so the model agrees with the formula it borrows.
  • Foot: ball of the foot over the pedal axle, about 13° toes-down at the bottom of the stroke, plus 15 mm for shoe and pedal.
  • Hands rest on the hoods, 110 mm ahead of and 30 mm above the bar clamp, with elbows bent 15°. The shoulder is placed where the torso (shoulder height minus hip height) and the arm meet.
  • Cockpit: 10 mm headset cover, the chosen spacers, a 40 mm tall stem clamp, a −6° stem, and a stem length of 70, 80, 90, or 100 mm for riders under 160, 170, 180, or 180 cm and over. Cranks are 165, 170, 172.5, or 175 mm for riders under 160, 170, 185, or 185 cm and over.

Flagging sizes that look too small or too large

The flags were calibrated against the rider height ranges that six manufacturers publish for 48 sizes of 9 bikes, listed in data/manufacturer_size_ranges.csv (Trek Checkpoint SL; Canyon Grail CF and Grizl CF; Devinci Hatchet Pro and Hatchet Vista; Salsa Cutthroat C and Flyway C; Lauf Seigla Rigid; BMC URS). For heights from 145 to 205 cm, each size was labelled “recommended” (inside the range), “too small” (at least 6 cm taller than the range), or “too large” (at least 6 cm shorter). The final rule:

  • Too small: hoods more than 3.8% of height below the saddle.
  • Too large: hoods more than 5.5% of height above the saddle, saddle to hoods more than 51% of height, exposed seatpost under 7% of height, or published standover more than 20 mm above inseam plus 25 mm of shoe.

Re-fitting the thresholds with each bike left out and testing on that bike, the rule flagged 7.4% of recommended sizes, caught 86% of too-small and 82% of too-large sizes, and never flagged in the wrong direction. Torso and shoulder angles were tried and dropped: they change little between sizes because the arms and torso adapt.

Limitations

  • Manufacturers measure some things differently. Seat tube length and standover in particular have no single definition, so compare them across brands with care.
  • Reference tyres differ. Published trail and BB height depend on the tyre each manufacturer assumed, and few say which.
  • Charts change. Values were read on one date and may since have been updated.
  • The rider is an estimate. Real bodies vary widely around average proportions, and flexibility matters as much as length.
  • No fit advice. Geometry is one part of fit. A bike’s stem, spacers, bars, and saddle setup change the rider’s position as much as the frame does.
